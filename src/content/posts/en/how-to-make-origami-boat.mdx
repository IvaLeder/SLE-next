- A decorative **sailboat**, which looks great but tips easily in water
- The **classic floating paper boat**, which has a wider, more stable hull

**Quick answer:** for a boat that really floats, use a rectangular A4 sheet and follow option 2. It takes about 10 minutes and needs no glue or scissors. The sailboat in option 1 is best used as a standing model or decoration.

## Paper boat instructions

### Materials needed

- Plain **A4** paper
- Your hands! :)
- Bonus: colored pencils for decoration

<Callout type="tip" title="Bigger paper, bigger boat">
The same folds work at any size. A sheet of newspaper makes a giant boat that carries far more cargo, while origami paper makes a neat little one. Making the same model at two sizes is a lovely way to see that folding is about proportions, not measurements.
</Callout>

### Option 1: how to make a paper sailboat

This model starts with a **square** sheet. If you have A4 paper, trim it to a square first. Keep the diagram beside you because the arrows show which layer moves at each fold.

1. Crease the square through the horizontal and vertical centres, then unfold it.
2. Fold the upper-left and lower-right corners into the centre, as shown in step 1 of the diagram.
3. Fold the model in half along the horizontal centre line.
4. Bring the two lower outside corners diagonally up to the top centre to make a layered triangle.
5. Fold the right-hand flap behind along the dotted diagonal line.
6. Open the central pocket, bring its sides together and press the model flat into a diamond.
7. Fold the bottom point up, then lift and shape the larger sail.
8. Tuck the small sail inside the hull and fold the base behind so the boat can stand.

### Option 2: how to make a classic floating paper boat

Use **rectangular A4 paper** for this version. Make each crease sharp with a fingernail before moving to the next step.

1. Fold the paper in half from left to right, crease it and unfold. This marks the vertical centre.
2. Fold the paper in half from top to bottom and keep it folded, with the folded edge at the top.
3. Fold both top corners down to the centre line. You should now have a triangle with a strip of paper below it.
4. Fold the front bottom strip up against the triangle. Turn the model over and fold the other strip up too; tuck the little corner tabs around the sides.
5. Open the triangle from the bottom, bring its two outside corners together and flatten it into a diamond.
6. Fold the lower point of the front layer up to the top point. Turn it over and repeat on the back to make a triangle.
7. Open that triangle from the bottom and flatten it into a smaller diamond.
8. Hold the two top flaps and gently pull them apart. The boat will open between your hands.
9. Flatten the bottom, pull the sides into shape and widen the hull so the boat stands upright.

<Callout type="tip" title="If the boat will not open">
Return to the small diamond in step 7 and make sure its open edges point upward. Pull only the two top flaps, slowly and evenly. Once it opens, press the centre of the hull flat and spread the sides so the boat does not fall over.
</Callout>

## Put your boats to the test

You made two boats, so don't just look at them - **race them**. A bowl, the bathtub or a puddle is all you need, and suddenly this is a science activity.

<Prediction question="You add coins to your paper boat one at a time. What decides the exact moment it sinks?">
<PredictionOption correct="true">When it weighs more than the water it pushes aside</PredictionOption>
<PredictionOption>When exactly 10 coins are inside</PredictionOption>
<PredictionOption>When the paper changes colour</PredictionOption>
<PredictionResult>
The first one. A boat floats as long as it **pushes aside more water than it weighs** - that upward push is called **buoyancy**. Each coin adds weight; once the boat's total weight beats the weight of the water it displaces, down it goes. A wider hull pushes aside more water, so it can carry more coins before sinking.
</PredictionResult>
</Prediction>

- **Which one lasts longer?** Launch both and time them. The sailboat usually gives up first. Ask your child *why* before you tell them: it sits lower, and water creeps up the paper faster.
- **How much cargo can it carry?** Add coins one at a time and count how many go in before the boat sinks. This is **buoyancy**: the boat floats as long as it pushes aside more water than it weighs. The same idea makes an [orange float with its peel on and sink without it](/en/orange-density-experiment).
- **Can you waterproof it?** Rub the outside of the hull hard with a wax crayon before launching, then race it against an untreated boat. Wax repels water, so the paper takes much longer to go soggy.
- **Change one thing at a time.** Thicker paper, a wider hull, a folded-up rim - try one change per boat and see what actually helps. That's a fair test, and it's exactly how real hull design works.

## The symbolism of the boat

The boat, as an object of **discovery** and **travel**, has deep roots in cultures around the world. It appears in famous stories and myths such as Noah’s Ark and *The Odyssey*, sometimes representing a journey through life and sometimes a passage to the afterlife.

In **ancient Egypt**, boats carried the Sun across the sky and helped souls travel to the afterlife. In **ancient Greece**, the ferryman Charon carried the dead across the river Styx, which is why a coin was traditionally placed with the deceased to pay for the crossing. Flood stories involving Noah’s boat also appear in **Christian** and **Islamic** traditions.

Paper boats often **symbolize life**: they are fragile and do not float forever. At the same time, a small boat setting out across unpredictable water can represent **hope, innocence and courage**.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="How do you make an origami boat?">
Start with a rectangular sheet (plain A4 works). Fold it in half, bring the top corners to the centre, fold the bottom strips up on both sides, flatten the model into a diamond, fold it into a smaller diamond, then pull the top flaps apart and shape the hull. The numbered instructions and diagram above show every step.
</Question>
<Question question="Why does a paper boat float?">
Because of buoyancy. The boat pushes water out of the way, and the water pushes back up with a force equal to the weight of the water displaced. As long as that upward push is at least as big as the boat's weight, the boat floats.
</Question>
<Question question="Why does a paper boat eventually sink?">
Two reasons: the paper slowly soaks up water and gets heavy, and any cargo adds weight. Once the total weight is greater than the weight of the water the boat can push aside, buoyancy loses and the boat sinks.
</Question>
<Question question="How can you make a paper boat waterproof?">
Rub the outside of the hull firmly with a wax crayon or candle before launching. Wax repels water, so the paper takes much longer to go soggy. Race a waxed boat against an untreated one to see the difference for yourself.
</Question>
<Question question="What paper is best for a paper boat?">
Ordinary A4 works well and is easy to fold. Thicker paper lasts longer in water but is harder to crease, and newspaper makes a great big boat. It's a fun variable to test: try different papers and see which stays afloat longest.
</Question>
<Question question="What does a paper boat symbolize?">
Paper boats often symbolize life - fragile, fleeting, and unable to float forever - but also hope and innocence, as the little boat bravely sets off against the odds. Boats appear across many cultures and myths, from Noah's Ark to the ferry across the river Styx.
</Question>
</FAQ>
