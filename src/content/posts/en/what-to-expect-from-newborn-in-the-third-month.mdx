### Importance of tummy time

Let’s start with the dreaded **tummy time**! Is there a baby who enjoys it? And yet, you know how important it is to exercise those little neck muscles. At 3 months, a baby should spend at least 20 minutes daily on his/her tummy. Easier said than done! Trying to lift their head is really hard for babies - it’s still very big compared to their body! You can see how much they are struggling to keep balance. Not only is this position quite uncomfortable, it’s also unfamiliar.

<Figure src="/images/posts/What-to-expect-from-newborn-in-the-Third-month-Tummy-Time.jpg" alt="Papercut illustration of a baby on her tummy lifting her head toward a gold star - tummy time building neck and shoulder strength at three months">
  Tummy time builds the neck muscles: aim for at least 20 minutes a day at 3 months.
</Figure>

Tummy time as a term is quite a new phenomenon. Parents used to put their babies to sleep on their tummy because they feared child could choke on their back. They were on tummy a lot, so there wasn’t a need for dedicated tummy time. But sleeping on the tummy proved unsafe and the **“back to sleep”** campaign advised parents to put children on their backs to sleep to avoid SIDS (Sudden infant death syndrome). Today, children are mostly on their backs (they do sleep a lot!), so it’s important to keep track of their tummy time.

But why is that important? **Motor skills** develop rapidly during the first year of life and they do so in certain order. You can’t walk before you crawl - although there are some exceptions. **Head control** is one of the most foundational milestones of the motor development. With proper head control, a child can learn how to roll, sit and eventually crawl and walk. Being on the tummy allows baby to experiment with new types of movements. First, there will be turning head from side to side, then lifting the head, then supporting with arms and lifting the whole chest… As with every skill, you must practice a lot to become a master.

If baby doesn’t like being on the tummy there are certain things you could try. First, try to limit duration. Make it **gradual** - everyone needs time to adjust to the new sensations. First weeks 1-2 minutes per day are great! Don’t try to do a tummy time when the baby is hungry, tired or upset. Also, wait for some time after the meal. If a baby is protesting, try to amuse her with rattles and toys, or even better, join her on the floor! If she is crying, pick her up and comfort her. You want to keep this a positive experience. With time, the baby will be able to stay on her tummy longer and hopefully even enjoy it.

### Oral stage and mouth exploration

You probably noticed from the start that your little one really likes to put things in their mouth. You only needed a breast, bottle or a pacifier to keep him/her satisfied. But as the time goes, and your baby finds out that hands are something she can control, get ready for more stuff ending up in their mouth.

Oral stage of development was first defined by Sigmund Freud and his psychoanalysis theory.

The theory says that every person goes through **5 psychosexual development stages** where the **oral stage** is the first one.

Oral stage lasts from the **birth** to the age of **18 months**. We won’t focus too much on psychosexual development here, but it’s important to notice that oral phase is here to stay for quite some time. (A side note: Freud’s psychosexual stages are a piece of psychology history rather than modern science, but the observation that babies explore the world with their mouths stands on its own.)

As your child develops hand-eye coordination, everything she grabs will end up in her mouth eventually. Why is that? As we mentioned earlier, almost everything is still new to your child and they needs to **explore** those strange new things. Texture, shape, smell, taste, so many interesting properties! But their tactile receptors on the hands are still not as developed as their mouth receptors. So putting things in the mouth lets your baby explore things to the fullest.

<Figure src="/images/posts/What-to-expect-from-newborn-in-the-Third-month-Oral-Phase.jpg" alt="Papercut illustration of a plum rattle and a wooden teething ring with soft sparkles - the oral phase when a three-month-old mouths everything">
  In the oral stage, everything within grabbing distance ends up in the mouth.
</Figure>

Another reason is **emotional regulation**. Your baby calms herself by putting things in their mouth and you probably know this after the first two months. That’s why pacifiers work so great - because the act of sucking calms down the baby. If your child doesn’t use a pacifier, they will find another way to soothe themselves - probably by sucking their hand.

The third reason can be **teeth growth**. The first tooth emerges at 6 months on average, but for some children, it can be much sooner, even at 3 months. If that is the case, expect edginess, irritability, drooling and a lot of chewing. You can help your child by giving them something to chew on: a teething ring or a cold wet cloth can help with the discomfort.

So now you know that putting things in the mouth is perfectly normal although annoying most of the times. There will be saliva everywhere, unpleasant sounds and obsessive behavior, not to mention worries about germs and hygiene. Still, it’s a phase that will pass. At least, that’s what we hope for. For our baby girl, it’s in full swing! We are trying to keep her hands and the environment reasonably clean, but it’s been challenging.

### More awareness = More interactions

One other big change you will notice in the third month of baby's life is that they are seeking a lot more attention and playtime. As the baby becomes more aware of their surroundings, they will become fascinated by new visual/auditory/tactile inputs.

As the sight improves, you’ll notice that your baby is staring at the new things they see in the environment, especially some patterns like shadows on the wall or sources of light. Also, they will start noticing the television and that can be a good attention grabber (although not a recommended one - official guidance is no screens at all before 18 months, video calls aside).

One other thing that we finally put to good use is our [homemade sensory bottle](/en/homemade-sensory-bottle-child-sensory-development). Our daughter really started to appreciate and enjoy its effects and playing with it can really grab her attention.

The same thing with sounds. You'll learn what music your baby likes and what isn’t their cup of tea. Of course, the sound of its parent's voice is usually the most preferred sound so talk to your baby and sing a lot. It's a great way to bond and it will help your baby develop language skills.

Also, try to avoid loud and sudden noises because you could startle your child. Your child is developing the sense of fear and there are no worse fear triggers than sudden and loud noises. Some other emotional responses can also be triggered by unexpected sounds. For example, roleplaying with different toys and mimicking different voices can really make them laugh. And by laugh, I mean real Laugh out Loud. It's quite the experience when you hear it for the first time!

With all the visual and auditory interaction, don’t forget about tactile stimulation and play. You probably already have a full house of toys made out of different materials. By toys, we mean everything from plushies, blankets, carboard, rattles, pots and pans... So put them to good use. Remember that your baby still takes most of the information from touching things. So let them feel different objects on their skin, in their hands and, inevitably, in their mouth.

So these are the biggest changes we noticed in the third month of our child’s life. There is lots of other stuff like constantly finding new methods to comfort them because effective strategies come and go (and sometimes come back), but we already talked about it [in the second month of child life](/en/what-to-expect-from-newborn-in-the-second-month).
