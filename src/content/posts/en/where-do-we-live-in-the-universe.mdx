Ask a five-year-old where they live and you will get the name of their street. Ask again and you get the town, then the country. Keep going and, sooner or later, somebody adds "and then Earth, and then space".

That last bit usually gets treated as a joke ending. It should not be. There are real steps above your country, they have names, and they come in a fixed order. This is your full address, and it is worth knowing where the lines run out.

<Callout type="info" title="🧒 In one sentence">
You live on Earth, which goes round the Sun, which is one of a few hundred billion stars in the Milky Way, which is one of about eighty galaxies in the Local Group, which is one small patch of an observable Universe 93 billion light years across.
</Callout>

## The whole address in one picture

Seven steps from you to everything. Each rung is far bigger than the one before it, and the last jump is the one nobody can really picture.

Now let us climb it one rung at a time.

## 1. You

Every measurement has to start somewhere, so it may as well start with your explorer. Call it a metre or so, standing up.

Keep this number in mind. Everything that follows is measured in multiples of things you can see, until quite suddenly it is not.

## 2. Earth

Our planet is **12,742 km** across. If you could drive without stopping at motorway speed, going all the way round the equator would take about three weeks.

That feels enormous, and for a walking animal it is. But Earth is a rocky ball of ordinary size, and it is not even the biggest thing in its own neighbourhood. It is not close.

## 3. The Solar System

One star, eight planets, a few dwarf planets, a great many moons, and a lot of ice and rubble.

Almost all of it is empty. This is the fact that surprises people most, and the reason a solar-system model on a poster is so misleading: to fit on the page, the planets have to be drawn hundreds of times too close together. If you shrank the Sun to the size of a football, Earth would be a peppercorn about 25 metres away, and Neptune a grain of rice three quarters of a kilometre down the road.

Light, which crosses your bedroom in less time than you can measure, needs about **8 minutes** to reach us from the Sun, and roughly **4 hours** more to reach Neptune.

## 4. Our star neighbourhood

Step outside the Solar System and the first thing you meet is a very great deal of nothing.

The nearest star to the Sun is **Proxima Centauri**, and it is 4.2 light years away. In the football-sized-Sun model from a moment ago, where Neptune was three quarters of a kilometre off, Proxima Centauri would be about **6,500 kilometres** away. Stars are not crowded. They are specks separated by oceans.

Within about 30 light years of us there are a few hundred stars. That is our neighbourhood, and every single one of them is unreachable with anything we can currently build.

## 5. The Milky Way

Now zoom out until those few hundred stars become a smudge.

The **Milky Way** is our galaxy: a slowly turning spiral of a few hundred billion stars, held together by gravity, about **100,000 light years** across. Light itself, the fastest thing there is, would need a hundred thousand years to cross it.

Two things about our address here are worth pausing on.

First, we are **not in the middle**. The Sun sits about 26,000 light years out from the centre, roughly halfway to the edge, on a smallish arm. It is an ordinary suburb.

Second, the whole thing is **turning**, and we are going round with it at about 220 kilometres every second. One full lap takes the Sun roughly 230 million years. The last time our Solar System was in this spot on its orbit, the first dinosaurs were just appearing.

## 6. The Local Group

Galaxies keep company. Ours belongs to a cluster of about **80 galaxies** called the Local Group, spread across roughly 10 million light years.

Most of them are small. Two are not: our Milky Way, and **Andromeda**, which is bigger than us and 2.5 million light years away. On a clear dark night, far from town, Andromeda is faintly visible to the naked eye as a smudge of light. It is the most distant thing most people will ever see without a telescope, and the light landing in your eye left it 2.5 million years ago, before our own species existed.

Andromeda is also heading straight for us at about 110 kilometres per second. In roughly four billion years the two galaxies will merge. Nobody needs to worry: with stars so far apart, almost nothing will actually collide.

## 7. The observable Universe

Here is where the ladder stops, and the reason it stops is worth understanding, because it is the best idea in the whole article.

The Universe is about **13.8 billion years old**. Light travels fast, but not infinitely fast, so light from anything too far away simply has not had time to reach us yet. There is a boundary, and it is not the edge of the Universe. It is the edge of **how much of it we can possibly see**.

That bubble around us is called the **observable Universe**, and it holds something like two trillion galaxies.

<Callout type="science" title="🔬 Why 93 billion and not 27.6 billion?">
Sharp explorers spot a problem. If the Universe is 13.8 billion years old, the bubble should be 13.8 billion light years in every direction, so 27.6 billion across. Yet astronomers say 93 billion. The reason is that **space itself has been stretching** the whole time that light has been travelling. The light took 13.8 billion years to get here, but the galaxy that emitted it has been carried much further away in the meantime. Both numbers are correct: one measures travel time, the other measures where things are now.
</Callout>

And the loveliest part: **everyone is at the centre of their own observable Universe**. A creature in a galaxy far away has its own bubble, its own edge, and sees us near its rim. Nobody is in the middle of everything, and everybody is in the middle of their own view. Both are true at once.

## Reading the address backwards

Try it out loud with your explorer, largest to smallest, and watch the moment it lands:

> The observable Universe, the Local Group, the Milky Way, the Orion Arm, the Solar System, Earth, your country, your town, your street, your house, you.

Eleven lines. Every one of them is a real place. The first three are things nobody had names for a few hundred years ago, and the whole idea that our galaxy is not the entire Universe is younger than most people's great-grandparents: it was settled in the 1920s.

That is the part I would want a child to take away. The address is enormous, yes. But it is also newly discovered, and there is nothing at all to say we have written the last line of it.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Your full address has real steps above your country: **Earth, the Solar System, the Milky Way, the Local Group, the observable Universe**.
- Earth is **12,742 km** across; the Solar System is mostly **empty space**, which posters always hide.
- A **light year** is the distance light travels in one year, and it makes distance and time the same measurement.
- The nearest star, **Proxima Centauri**, is 4.2 light years away. Stars are specks separated by enormous gaps.
- The **Milky Way** is about **100,000 light years** across, and we sit about 26,000 light years from the centre, not in the middle.
- The **Local Group** holds about 80 galaxies; **Andromeda** is the big one, 2.5 million light years away and visible to the naked eye.
- The **observable Universe** is 93 billion light years across, and it is a limit on **what we can see**, not the edge of everything.
- Because space has stretched while light travelled, the bubble is wider than the Universe's age in light years would suggest.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="What is our full address in the Universe?">
Working outwards: your house, your street, your town, your country, **Earth**, the **Solar System**, the **Orion Arm**, the **Milky Way**, the **Local Group**, and the **observable Universe**. Each step is a real, named structure held together, in the larger cases, by gravity.
</Question>
<Question question="What is a light year?">
The distance light travels in one year: about **9.5 trillion kilometres**. It is a measure of distance, not time, despite the name. It is useful because it also tells you how old the view is. Something 100 light years away appears to you as it was 100 years ago.
</Question>
<Question question="Where is Earth in the Milky Way?">
About **26,000 light years** from the centre, roughly halfway out to the rim, on a minor arm called the Orion Arm or Orion Spur. We are neither central nor on the edge, which is one reason the night sky shows a band of stars in one direction: that is us looking along the disc of our own galaxy.
</Question>
<Question question="How big is the observable Universe?">
About **93 billion light years** across, holding an estimated two trillion galaxies. The word "observable" is doing real work: it is the region whose light has had time to reach us in 13.8 billion years. What lies beyond it may well go on for ever. We simply cannot see it.
</Question>
<Question question="Is the Universe bigger than the observable Universe?">
Almost certainly yes, and possibly infinitely so. The observable part is a bubble centred on us and limited by the speed of light and the age of the Universe. It says something about our view, not about where the Universe stops.
</Question>
<Question question="Are we at the centre of the Universe?">
We are at the centre of *our* observable Universe, and so is everybody else at the centre of theirs. That sounds contradictory until you picture each observer with their own bubble of visible space. There is no known centre to the Universe as a whole.
</Question>
<Question question="How do I explain these distances to a young child?">
Shrink everything. Make the Sun a football, and Earth becomes a peppercorn 25 metres away. Better still, take a roll of toilet paper into the hallway and pace out the planets one square at a time. Children reliably guess that the planets are close together, and the moment they discover otherwise is worth more than any number.
</Question>
</FAQ>
