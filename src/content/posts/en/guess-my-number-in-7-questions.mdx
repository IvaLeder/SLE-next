Here is a trick you can do in a car, in a queue, or at a birthday party.

Ask someone to think of a number from 1 to 100 and keep it secret. Tell them you will find it in seven questions, and that all they have to answer is "higher" or "lower".

Then ask: **is it 50?**

Higher. Ask 75. Higher. Ask 88. Higher. Ask 94. Lower. Ask 91. Lower. Ask 89. Higher. There is only one number left that fits every answer, so it is 90, and that was your seventh question.

It works every time, for every number, and it never needs an eighth question. It also happens to be one of the most useful ideas in computer science, and a child who plays this game for ten minutes has understood it properly.

## The rule: always aim for the middle

The whole method is one sentence: **guess the middle of whatever is still possible.**

At the start, anything from 1 to 100 is possible, so the middle is 50. If the answer is "higher", then 1 to 50 are all gone at once, and you are left with 51 to 100. The middle of that is 75. If the answer is "lower", 76 to 100 disappear, and you are down to 51 to 74.

Each answer throws away half the numbers, whichever way it goes. That is the part worth saying out loud, because it is where children usually expect the trick to have a catch: it does not matter what the answer is. Both answers are equally useful.

Watch the pile shrink:

| Questions asked | Numbers that could still be right |
|---|---|
| 0 | 100 |
| 1 | 50 |
| 2 | 25 |
| 3 | 13 |
| 4 | 7 |
| 5 | 4 |
| 6 | 2 |
| 7 | 1 |

Seven halvings take 100 possibilities down to one. There is no room left for an eighth question, because after seven there is nothing left to be unsure about.

## Play it the other way round

Once your child has watched you do it, swap. You think of the number and let them find it, and resist the urge to coach.

What almost every child does first is guess 1, then 2, then 3. This is worth letting them do for a while, because the tedium is the lesson. Counting up from 1 needs up to 100 guesses. Then ask them to try starting in the middle, and let them feel the difference.

The moment to watch for is when they stop guessing "a number" and start guessing "the middle of what is left". Those are two completely different games, and the second one is an **algorithm**: a recipe that works for any number without needing luck.

## Why doubling is so powerful

The numbers in that prediction are the powers of two: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256… Add one question and the pile you can search doubles.

Turn it around and you get the rule: to search a pile of any size, you need the number of questions that gets you past the size by doubling. 100 needs 7, because 2⁷ = 128. A thousand needs 10. A million needs 20. A billion needs only 30.

Your child has met this doubling before if they have played with the [Tower of Hanoi](/en/make-and-solve-tower-of-hanoi), where each extra disk doubles the number of moves, or if they have [written their name in binary](/en/write-your-name-in-binary), where each extra bit doubles how many letters you can spell. It is the same engine driving all three.

<Callout type="science" title="Where the halving trick hides in your day">
When you look up a word in a paper dictionary, you do not start at A. You open it somewhere near the middle, see whether your word comes before or after, and pick a side. That is binary search, done with a thumb. The same goes for finding a page number in a thick book, and for the way a programmer hunts a bug by checking the middle of a list of changes rather than replaying all of them.
</Callout>

## The catch that makes it computer science

There is one condition, and it is the real lesson of the whole activity. Ask your child:

> Could you play this game if the numbers were shuffled up, so that "higher" and "lower" meant nothing?

You could not. Halving only works because the numbers are **in order**. The answer "higher" is only useful if it lets you rule out everything below, and that needs the pile to be sorted.

This is why computers spend so much effort keeping things in order. A phone with 2,000 contacts sorted by name can find one in **11** comparisons. Unsorted, it might have to look at all 2,000. Sorting a list once, so that every later search can be a binary search, is one of the most common trades in all of software.

<Callout type="tip" title="🔬 Turn it into a real experiment">
Test the condition instead of taking our word for it. Write the numbers 1 to 20 on cards, shuffle them, and lay them face down in a row. Now try to find the card with 13 on it, with the only allowed question being "is this higher or lower than 13?" Count the flips: on average you will turn over about half the cards, and sometimes all of them. Then lay the same cards out in order, face down, and play again. Five flips at most, every time. Same cards, same question, and the only thing that changed was the order.
</Callout>

## Playing it away from a screen

**A number line on paper.** Draw 1 to 100 as a line and cross out the half that each answer eliminates. Seeing the surviving stretch get physically shorter does more for a young child than any amount of explaining.

**The dictionary race.** You look up a word by starting at A and turning one page at a time. Your child looks up a different word by opening the middle and halving. Race. It is not close.

**Guess the age, guess the weight.** The same trick finds anything with an order to it: how many sweets are in the jar, how many pages in the book, what number I am thinking of between 1 and 1000.

**Twenty questions, properly.** The classic game is binary search on ideas rather than numbers, and a good question is one that splits the possibilities roughly in half. "Is it an animal?" is a strong question. "Is it a hamster?" is a weak one. That is a genuinely useful thing for a child to notice about questions.

<KeyTakeaways>
- To find a secret number from 1 to 100, always guess the **middle** of what is still possible. It takes at most **7** questions.
- Every answer removes **half** the remaining possibilities, no matter which answer it is.
- Each extra question **doubles** the size of the pile you can search: 7 questions cover 128 numbers, 10 cover 1024, 20 cover over a million.
- This is called **binary search**, and it only works when the things you are searching are **in order**. That is why computers sort things.
- The same halving finds a word in a dictionary, a page in a book, and a bug in a program.
- Guessing 1, 2, 3, 4 in turn is the honest comparison: up to 100 guesses instead of 7.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Guessing games and binary search - Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="How can you always guess a number from 1 to 100 in 7 tries?">
Always guess the middle of the range that is still possible. Start at 50. If the answer is "higher", the range becomes 51 to 100 and you guess 75; if "lower", it becomes 1 to 49 and you guess 25. Each answer halves the range: 100, 50, 25, 13, 7, 4, 2, 1. After seven halvings only one number can be left, so seven questions are always enough.
</Question>
<Question question="What is binary search in simple terms?">
It is a way of finding something in an ordered list by repeatedly cutting the list in half. Look at the middle item, decide whether what you want is before or after it, throw the other half away, and repeat. "Binary" means two, because every step splits things into two parts and discards one.
</Question>
<Question question="How many guesses do you need for 1 to 1000?">
Ten. Every extra question doubles the amount you can search, so the coverage grows 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024. Ten questions cover more than a thousand numbers. For a million you need only 20, and for a billion 30.
</Question>
<Question question="Why does binary search need a sorted list?">
Because the answers only help if they let you rule out a whole half at once. "Higher" tells you nothing about a shuffled pile: the number you want could still be anywhere. In an ordered list, "higher" eliminates everything below the item you checked. No order, no halving, and you are back to looking at things one by one.
</Question>
<Question question="Is this the same as the game 20 questions?">
It is the same idea applied to ideas rather than numbers. Twenty yes-or-no questions can separate more than a million possibilities, as long as each question splits what is left roughly in half. That is why "is it alive?" is a much better opening question than "is it a goldfish?".
</Question>
<Question question="What age can children learn binary search?">
The guessing game itself works from about 5 or 6, played out loud with an adult doing the halving. Around 7 or 8 most children can find the middle themselves and stick to the strategy. The reasoning about why doubling makes 7 enough for 100, and the fact that the list must be sorted, usually clicks between 9 and 12.
</Question>
<Question question="How is binary search used in real programs?">
Constantly, wherever data is kept in order. Databases use sorted indexes so a search touches a handful of records instead of millions. Version control tools find the change that broke a program by testing the middle of the history and halving. Autocomplete narrows a sorted word list. Even a spell checker leans on it.
</Question>
</FAQ>
