<Callout type="info" title="🧒 In one sentence">
Fears are a normal, even healthy part of growing up: they change as a child's mind develops, and most fade with time, patience and small brave steps.
</Callout>

The vacuum cleaner. The dark hallway. A dog on the other side of the street. The neighbour's Halloween mask. Almost every child goes through phases of being afraid of something, and for parents it can be hard to know what's normal, what helps and what accidentally makes it worse.

The good news: most childhood fears are **a sign of healthy development**, not a problem. They appear in a surprisingly predictable order, because each new fear reflects something new the child's brain has just learned to understand. Below you'll find the common fears at each age, why they show up, and practical ways to help.

## Why do children have fears?

Fear is the brain's alarm system. It keeps us away from danger, and in small children it does an important job: it keeps them close to their grown-ups while they're still learning what's safe.

What a child fears depends on what they can understand. A baby can't imagine monsters, but can notice a sudden loud bang. A four-year-old can imagine almost anything, but can't yet fully tell imagination from reality. A ten-year-old knows monsters aren't real, but now understands that people can get ill, that tests can be failed and that classmates can laugh. **New fears follow new thinking.**

## Common fears at each age

Every child is different, and the ages below are just guidelines. Some children skip a fear entirely, others show it earlier or later.

| Age | Common fears | Why now? |
|---|---|---|
| 0-12 months | Loud noises, sudden movements, falling, strangers (from about 6-8 months), separation (from about 8-10 months) | The baby starts to tell familiar faces from unfamiliar ones and realises that a parent who leaves still exists somewhere else |
| 1-2 years | Separation, strangers, loud machines (vacuum, hand dryer, toilet flush), the bath drain, doctors | Strong attachment, a growing sense of being small in a big world, but little understanding of how things work |
| 3-5 years | The dark, monsters and ghosts, masks and costumes, being alone at bedtime, animals (especially dogs), bad dreams, thunder | A rich imagination without a firm line between fantasy and reality |
| 6-8 years | Getting hurt, getting lost, burglars, storms and natural disasters, scary news, death (of a pet or a loved one), failing at school | Understanding real-world dangers and that death is permanent, but not yet how rare most dangers are |
| 9-12 years | Tests and grades, embarrassment, being left out, illness, world events (war, climate, news) | Social awareness and comparing themselves to others, plus a growing awareness of the wider world |

### Babies (0-12 months)

Newborns startle at loud noises and at the feeling of falling. These are reflexes, not fears in the grown-up sense. Around 6 to 8 months many babies start to be wary of **strangers**: they suddenly cry when a friendly aunt reaches for them. A few months later comes **separation anxiety**, when a baby protests every time a parent leaves the room. Both are actually positive: they mean the baby has formed a strong bond and can tell "my people" from everyone else.

### Toddlers (1-2 years)

Separation anxiety often peaks between about 10 and 18 months and then slowly eases. Toddlers are also famously afraid of **loud machines**: the vacuum cleaner, the blender, the hand dryer in a public toilet. They don't yet know what these things do, only that they are loud and scary. Some toddlers fear the bath drain, worrying they might go down it too. They really can't yet judge sizes and how things work, so the fear makes sense from their point of view.

### Preschoolers (3-5 years)

This is the golden age of **monsters under the bed**. Imagination is exploding, and when it's dark and quiet, the mind is full of possibilities. Preschoolers also commonly fear dogs and other animals, thunderstorms, bad dreams and being alone at bedtime.

Here's one that surprises many parents: **masks and costumes**. A preschooler may know that it's their big brother under the werewolf mask, and still be frightened, because part of them isn't quite sure the face hasn't really changed. Respect that fear: it's real for them.

### Early school age (6-8 years)

As imaginary fears fade, **realistic** ones take their place: getting hurt, getting lost, burglars, fires, storms. Children of this age are starting to understand that death is permanent and happens to everyone, which can bring new worries about pets, grandparents and parents. They also notice the news far more than adults realise. School brings worries about doing well and fitting in.

### Older children (9-12 years)

Fears become more **social and performance-based**: tests, speaking in front of the class, being laughed at, not being invited. Many children also worry about health and world events. These worries are more abstract, so they often show up as questions at bedtime, stomach aches before school, or "what if..." conversations.

## How to help your child with fears

### 1. Take the fear seriously

"There's nothing to be scared of!" is the most natural thing to say, and one of the least helpful. To the child, the fear is very real. Name it instead: *"You're scared of the dog. Its bark is really loud."* Feeling understood calms the body. Then you can add a little perspective: *"I'm here, and this dog is on a lead."*

### 2. Stay calm and show them how it's done

Children constantly check their grown-ups' faces to decide whether something is dangerous. If you stay relaxed around the dog, the thunder or the dentist's chair, you send a powerful message: *this is safe.*

Children also learn bravery by watching it. If a fear is yours too (spiders, anyone?), you don't have to hide it. Say it out loud and show them what you do with it: *"I'm a bit scared of spiders, so I'm going to take a deep breath and carry this one outside in a glass."* Seeing a grown-up feel scared and cope anyway is one of the most powerful lessons a child can get.

### 3. Small brave steps, not big leaps

Avoiding everything scary feels kind, but it teaches the brain that the fear was right. Pushing a child to face a fear all at once ("Just pet the dog!") can backfire too. That's called flooding: it causes a lot of stress, often makes the fear stronger and can damage the child's trust in you. Professionals use it only rarely, with specialist training and the person's consent. What works best sits in between: **small, gradual steps**, each one only a little scary, with lots of praise for trying.

For a fear of dogs, the steps might be: look at dog pictures in a book, watch a dog from across the park, stand near a calm dog on a lead, touch a relaxed, friendly dog on the back together with a grown-up (with the owner's permission, and only if the dog is clearly happy to be touched), and finally pet it. Add as many steps in between as your child needs, and it's fine to stay on one step for a while. The goal doesn't have to be the top of the ladder, either: for many children, walking calmly past a dog is good enough. Your child sets the pace; you cheer them on. Psychologists call this a **fear ladder**, and it's the heart of how fears are treated professionally, too.

### 4. Explain how things work

Knowledge shrinks fears. Show your toddler the vacuum cleaner while it's switched off and let them press the button. Explain to your five-year-old where thunder comes from. Read books about the thing they're afraid of. A fear of the unknown often melts once it becomes known.

### 5. Teach tools for the body and mind

- **Calm breathing:** "smell the flower" (breathe in through the nose), "blow out the candle" (breathe out slowly through the mouth). Three times.
- **Brave words:** a short sentence the child can say to themselves: *"I can do hard things."* *"Scary isn't the same as dangerous."*
- **A comfort object:** a soft toy, a torch, a photo of the family in a pocket.
- **A worry thermometer:** ask "how big is the worry, from 1 to 5?" It helps children notice that fear goes up and down, and it lets you see progress.

### 6. Praise bravery, not the absence of fear

Being brave doesn't mean not being scared. It means doing something **while** scared. Tell them: *"You were scared, and you went into the dark room anyway. That's brave!"*

### 7. Play and stories help, especially for little ones

Preschoolers work through fears through play. Let them be the doctor who gives the teddy an injection, or the vet who examines the scary dog. Draw the monster together and give it a silly hat, a funny voice or whatever else makes your child giggle. Laughter is a great antidote to fear, so take every chance to laugh together.

Some families use a "monster spray" at bedtime: a spray bottle of plain water with a homemade label. That works for some young children; others take it as proof that monsters exist. Follow your child: if they're older or the spray adds new questions, gently move to "monsters are pretend" and a nightlight.

### 8. Give the fear a name

Many children find a fear easier to handle once it has a name and a face, like Mr Dark or the Worry Monster. Turning the fear into a separate character helps a child see it as *something they have*, not *who they are*, and gives you both a way to talk about it. Draw the worry monster together, then draw new versions of it: scary, silly, friendly. Changing how it looks helps children feel more in control.

Explain that the monster is trying to protect them, but sometimes it overreacts and sounds the alarm when there's no real danger. The more we listen to it and avoid things, the bigger and bossier it gets. Then ask: *"What is the Worry Monster telling you about school today?"* and *"What could we do to make it a little smaller?"*

Older children often enjoy the real science: fear is the brain's reaction to something it *thinks* is a threat, and a small part of the brain called the amygdala sometimes sounds the alarm too quickly. A simple brain diagram can make this click.

### 9. Watch what they watch

Young children, roughly under 7, often can't judge how real something on screen is, or how likely it is to happen to them. Check age ratings, watch together when you can, and keep news programmes, especially about war or disasters, away from young children. If older children have already seen something frightening, ask what they understood and fill in the gaps honestly and calmly.

## What not to do

- **Don't mock or shame** ("Big boys aren't scared of the dark!"). Shame adds a second problem on top of the fear.
- **Don't use fear as a threat.** "The bogeyman will take you if you don't behave" may work for a moment, but it plants a fear the child then has to carry, often at bedtime.
- **Don't force it all at once.** Throwing a child into the pool to "cure" a fear of water can backfire badly.
- **Don't rearrange your whole life around the fear.** Parents naturally protect: they sleep next to the child every night, skip every party with dogs, answer the same worried question twenty times. A little help is fine, but when the family's whole routine bends around the fear, it tends to grow. In a Yale study of children aged 7 to 14 with anxiety disorders, parents who learned to gently reduce this "accommodation" through a programme called SPACE, while staying warm and confident in their child, helped as much as therapy with the child did.

## Halloween and spooky season

Halloween is a perfect example of fears by age. The same decorations that thrill a nine-year-old can terrify a four-year-old.

- **Let the child choose their level.** Some children love skeletons, others want pumpkins and nothing more. Both are fine. (Our [Pumpkins & Potions lab](/en/pumpkins-and-potions) rates every activity with a "spook-o-meter" for exactly this reason.)
- **Practise with costumes at home.** Let the child watch a grown-up put the mask on and take it off, try it on themselves, look in the mirror.
- **Skip haunted houses and jump scares for preschoolers**, even if older siblings beg.
- **Have an exit plan.** If trick-or-treating gets too much, going home early is fine. Let the child know in advance that they can leave if they want to, and that it's OK to do so.
- **Turn spooky into science.** A "monster" that turns out to be a shadow puppet, or a "potion" that's really red cabbage juice, shows children that scary things often have ordinary explanations.

<Callout type="info" title="When to seek professional help?">
Most fears fade on their own. Talk to your paediatrician or a child psychologist if a fear is very intense or out of proportion, lasts for six months or more, or gets in the way of everyday life: sleep, nursery or school, friendships, family outings. Also ask for help if fear comes with panic attacks, frequent stomach aches or headaches without a medical cause, a return to outgrown behaviour (like bedwetting), or if it started after a frightening event such as an accident. Early support works well, and asking for it is a sign of good parenting, not of failure.
</Callout>

## A note for preschool teachers and educators

Group settings bring their own fears: drop-off, loud events, visitors in costume, fire drills. Warn children in advance ("Today a firefighter is visiting, in a big helmet"), let them see costumes going on and coming off, and give anxious children a job ("you hold the door"), because having a role makes it easier to stay focused. For seasonal celebrations, offer a non-scary option for every activity and never make costumes compulsory. And when a child is frightened, a calm, matter-of-fact adult can give them the support they need, without drawing too much attention to the fear.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Fears are a **normal part of development** and change in a predictable order as children's thinking grows.
- Babies fear noises and separation, preschoolers fear **monsters, the dark and masks**, and school-age children fear **real dangers and social situations**.
- Take fears seriously, stay calm, and help your child face them in **small, gradual steps**: a fear ladder.
- Praise **bravery**, which means acting while scared, not being fearless.
- Avoid shaming, forcing and using fear as a threat, and seek help if a fear is intense, lasting or gets in the way of daily life.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="What are the most common fears in childhood?">
The most common fears are of the dark, monsters and ghosts, animals (especially dogs), loud noises, being separated from parents, getting hurt, and, in older children, school performance and being left out. Which ones appear depends mostly on the child's age.
</Question>
<Question question="At what age is a fear of the dark most common?">
Fear of the dark usually appears around 2 to 3 years and is strongest between about 3 and 6, when imagination is rich but the line between pretend and real is still blurry. Most children grow out of it during the early school years.
</Question>
<Question question="Should I let my child avoid what they're afraid of?">
A little avoidance is fine, especially at first, but avoiding a fear completely tends to keep it going. Instead, help your child take small, gradual steps towards the feared thing, at their pace, and praise every brave try.
</Question>
<Question question="Is it OK to use a nightlight?">
Yes. A dim, warm nightlight is a simple and reasonable way to help a child who's afraid of the dark, and it doesn't stop them from outgrowing the fear. Choose a soft, warm light rather than a bright or blue one, so it doesn't disturb sleep.
</Question>
<Question question="What's the difference between nightmares and night terrors?">
A nightmare is a bad dream. It usually happens in the second half of the night, and the child wakes up, remembers it and wants comfort, so give it: a cuddle, a few calm words and, if they like, a chat about the dream in the morning. A night terror happens in deep sleep, usually in the first few hours after falling asleep. The child may scream, thrash or seem wide awake, but isn't really awake, doesn't respond to comfort and won't remember it in the morning. Don't try to wake them: stay close, keep them safe and wait for it to pass, usually within a few minutes. Night terrors are most common in preschool and early school age and are usually outgrown. If they happen often, talk to your paediatrician.
</Question>
<Question question="My preschooler is scared of Halloween masks. Is that normal?">
Yes, very. Preschoolers can't yet be fully sure that a mask doesn't change the person under it. Let them watch you put masks on and take them off, keep decorations gentle, and skip jump scares. The fear usually fades by around age six or seven.
</Question>
<Question question="When is a child's fear a sign of anxiety?">
When it's much stronger than other children of the same age experience, lasts for months, and gets in the way of sleep, school, play or family life. In that case, talk to your paediatrician or a child psychologist. Childhood anxiety responds well to support, especially when it starts early.
</Question>
</FAQ>
