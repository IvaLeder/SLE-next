It usually comes at an unexpected moment: in the car, at bedtime, or in the middle of writing a letter to the North Pole. "Mum, is Santa *real*?" And suddenly you're not sure what to say. Tell the truth and end the magic? Keep the story going? And what if your answer hurts their trust?

Here's what research tells us about when children stop believing, how they feel when they find out, and how to answer the big question in a way that's honest and kind.

## Is it OK for children to believe in Santa?

Yes. Believing in Santa, the Tooth Fairy or a monster under the bed is a normal part of how young children think. Between about 3 and 6, imagination is blossoming, and it can be hard to tell what's real and what's pretend. Children at this age often believe in magic and play with it at the same time: they can be completely sure Santa exists *and* happily pretend to be Santa themselves.

Psychologists who study children's beliefs generally don't see Santa as harmful. Pretend play and stories help children practise imagining other people's points of view, think about what's possible, and enjoy shared family rituals. The magic of Christmas morning is part of that.

You can also choose **not** to do Santa. Some families don't mention Santa at all. Some families prefer to frame it as a fun Christmas story. It can still be fun and magical, even though it's just pretend. You can even ask your children would they like to pretend that Santa is real and carry on with all those little rituals: letter, cookies, milk... One important thing to teach is respect: other children believe, and it's not your child's job to tell "the truth" to their classmates.

In the [classic study by Anderson and Prentice (1994)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02253287), most children discovered the truth by themselves at around age 7. A [more recent study by Mills and colleagues (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001662), which interviewed children and surveyed hundreds of adults about their memories, found an average of about 8, with a wide range on both sides.

What tips children off? Usually a mix of things they notice and things they're told:

- **Other people:** a friend at school, an older sibling or cousin.
- **Clues at home:** Santa's handwriting looks like Dad's, the wrapping paper is the same as Grandma's, a price tag is still on the box.
- **Too many Santas:** one in the shopping centre, one at kindergarten, one on TV, all in the same week.
- **Logic:** how does he visit every home in one night? How does he fit down a chimney, or get into a flat without one?

That last one is particularly interesting. [Shtulman and Yoo (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogdev.2014.12.006) found that children who were better at telling physically *impossible* events from merely unusual ones asked Santa more probing questions: not "What do elves eat?" but "How do you fit down the chimney?" Working out the truth about Santa is, in a way, a small victory of scientific thinking.

## How do children feel when they find out?

Better than most parents expect.

In the 1994 study, children described their reactions as mostly **positive**. They felt proud of figuring it out and grown-up for being let in on the secret. It was the **parents** who mostly described feeling sad.

The 2024 study found a more mixed picture: about a third of the children felt some disappointment or sadness, but fewer than a third said those feelings lasted more than a couple of weeks. Most children trusted their parents just as much as before.

Two things were linked to more upset:

- **Heavy promotion.** Children whose parents had pushed the Santa story hardest tended to feel more disappointed when they found out.
- **A sudden reveal from someone else, at an older age.** Adults who remembered the discovery as painful were more likely to have found out abruptly, later than their friends, and from another person rather than by working it out themselves.

The lesson for parents isn't "never do Santa". It's: don't over-invest in "proving" it, and let children reach the truth at their own pace, ideally with you.

## Signs your child is starting to wonder

- Their questions get more specific and practical: "How do the reindeer fly?", "Why does Santa use our wrapping paper?"
- They set little tests: comparing handwriting, staying awake, leaving a trap.
- They ask you straight out: "Are *you* Santa?"
- They mention what a friend or sibling has said.

A useful question to ask yourself is: **is my child asking to know, or asking to keep believing?** Some children ask "Is Santa real?" hoping to hear "Of course he is!" Others really want the truth. Think about the child in front of you.

## How to answer "Is Santa real?"

### 1. Answer with a question

"What do you think?" is the best first reply. It isn't a dodge: it tells you where your child is. A child who says "I think he's real, because he ate the biscuits!" is still enjoying the magic. A child who says "I think it's you" is ready for the truth.

### 2. Follow their lead

If your child is happily building theories (maybe Santa has helpers, maybe he stops time), you don't have to correct them. Curiosity is the point. Mills, one of the researchers behind the 2024 study, advises parents to follow their child's lead as they approach the typical age of doubting.

### 3. When they really want to know, tell the truth

If your child asks sincerely and directly, and especially if they ask more than once, tell them gently. Avoid inventing more and more elaborate "proof" to keep the story alive. That can make the eventual discovery feel more like being tricked.

You might say something like:

> "You've worked it out. Santa is a story that people all over the world tell, and mums and dads do the Santa part. We did it because it's fun and magical, and we loved watching you enjoy it. Now that you know, you get to be part of the secret."

### 4. Invite them into the secret

This is where the disappointment often turns into pride. A child who knows can become **Santa's helper**:

- Help fill a younger sibling's stocking.
- Choose a present for someone and give it anonymously, so the recipient never knows who it was from.
- Play Secret Santa in the family, with clues written in [secret code](/en/how-to-make-cipher-wheel).

The message is that Santa was never only about getting presents. He's about the joy of giving, and now your child is on the giving side.

### 5. Make room for big feelings

Some children feel sad or even tricked. Let them. "You're disappointed. That makes sense: it was something special." If your child feels lied to, you can acknowledge it simply: "You're right that we told you a story. We did it to make Christmas magical, never to trick you."

## Keeping the secret for younger siblings

Older children are usually delighted to keep the secret. It makes them feel trusted and grown-up. Talk about it together: "Your little brother still believes. Shall we keep the magic going for him?" Give them a real role, like helping to wrap presents or eating Santa's biscuits.

## If someone else spoils it

If a classmate announces that Santa isn't real, start the same way: "What do *you* think?" Your child may simply want to talk it through, or may decide to keep believing for one more year. Both are fine. If they want the truth, give it, kindly.

## Santa isn't the only gift-bringer

Across Europe, children wait for many different visitors. In Croatia, as in much of central Europe, many children get their first presents on **6 December** from **St Nicholas** (Sveti Nikola), who fills shoes or boots left on the windowsill. He's often accompanied by **Krampus**, a dark, scary figure who in the old stories leaves a golden twig for children who haven't been good. At Christmas, presents come from **Djed Božićnjak** (Father Christmas), or in some families from **Mali Isus**, the baby Jesus.

Children who grow up with several gift-bringers often start comparing notes early ("Why does St Nicholas bring small presents and Santa big ones?"), which can be a gentle way into the bigger question. And if Krampus worries your child, our guide to [childhood fears by age](/en/childhood-fears-by-age) has ideas for helping them feel brave.

## A note for teachers and educators

In a classroom, children will believe different things, and some families don't celebrate Christmas at all. If a child asks you whether Santa is real, a neutral answer works best: "Different families believe different things. What do you think? You could ask your family too." It's not your place to confirm or deny, and children are usually happy with that.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="At what age should you tell your child Santa isn't real?">
There's no single right age. Most children work it out themselves at around 7 or 8. Rather than picking a date, follow your child's lead: when they ask sincerely and directly, and want a real answer, it's time.
</Question>
<Question question="Is lying about Santa bad for children?">
Research suggests most children cope well when they find out and continue to trust their parents. Disappointment is more likely when parents push the story very hard or when the child finds out abruptly from someone else, so keep it playful and let the truth come gently. You can also choose not to do Santa at all, or to tell it as a magical story from the start.
</Question>
<Question question="What do you say when your child asks if Santa is real?">
Start with "What do you think?" to find out whether they want to keep believing or want the truth. If they want the truth, explain that Santa is a story parents tell to make Christmas magical, and invite them to help keep the secret for others.
</Question>
<Question question="How do I tell my child about Santa without ruining Christmas?">
Frame it as being let in on a secret. Give your child a role as Santa's helper: filling a sibling's stocking, choosing an anonymous gift, or playing Secret Santa. The story of Santa is magical and fun, and even though it's just pretend, it can still be a part of Christmas.
</Question>
<Question question="What if an older sibling tells a younger child the truth?">
Talk with the younger child calmly and ask what they think. Some decide to keep believing for a while. Then talk with the older child about the joy of keeping the secret and give them a special role for next year.
</Question>
<Question question="My child is upset after finding out. What should I do?">
Let them be sad, and say that it makes sense. Acknowledge the story honestly and explain that it was meant to make Christmas special, not to trick them. For most children, the disappointment passes fairly quickly.
</Question>
</FAQ>
