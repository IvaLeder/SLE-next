Look at the Moon on several nights and it seems unable to make up its mind. One evening it is a thin silver curve. A week later it is a bright half-circle. Then it grows round and full before slowly disappearing again.

The Moon itself has not changed shape. Nobody has taken a bite out of it, and Earth's shadow is not sliding across it. The Moon is still a ball, and the Sun is still lighting half of that ball. What changes is **how much of the bright half we can see from Earth**.

That one idea explains every Moon phase.

<Callout type="info" title="🧒 In one sentence">
The Sun always lights half of the Moon, and the phases happen because the Moon's orbit lets us see different amounts of that bright half.
</Callout>

## The most important half

The Moon does not make its own light. **Moonlight is reflected sunlight**, rather like the patch of light that bounces from a torch onto a pale wall.

At any ordinary moment, sunlight illuminates one half of the Moon while the other half has night. That remains true during a new Moon, a crescent, a quarter and a full Moon. The amount of the whole Moon in sunlight has not changed.

Our viewing angle has.

Imagine a lamp shining on a white ball. From beside the lamp you see the bright side of the ball. Walk around behind the ball and you see its dark side instead. The lamp has not changed and the ball has not changed. Only your position has.

With the real Moon, we stay on Earth while the Moon changes position. As it travels around us, we see a changing share of its sunlit half. Those changing views are the **Moon's phases**.

## The eight Moon phases in order

A phase changes continuously; the Moon does not jump between eight frozen shapes. We give names to eight useful points in the cycle so that we can describe what we see.

1. **New Moon.** The Moon is roughly between Earth and the Sun. Its bright half faces mostly away from us, so the side we see is dark and usually lost in the Sun's glare.
2. **Waxing crescent.** A narrow bright curve appears. **Waxing** means the illuminated part we see is growing.
3. **First quarter.** Half of the Moon's visible face looks bright. It is called a quarter because the Moon is about one quarter of the way through its cycle, not because only one quarter of the disk is lit.
4. **Waxing gibbous.** More than half looks bright, but the Moon is not full yet. **Gibbous** means rounded or bulging outward.
5. **Full Moon.** Earth is roughly between the Sun and Moon, and almost the whole Earth-facing side is illuminated.
6. **Waning gibbous.** The bright portion begins to shrink. **Waning** means getting smaller.
7. **Last quarter.** The other half of the visible face looks bright. The Moon is about three quarters of the way through its cycle.
8. **Waning crescent.** Only a narrowing curve remains before the cycle returns to new Moon.

<Callout type="science" title="🌍 Which side is bright?">
People everywhere experience the same phase at the same time, but they do not see it at the same angle. From the Southern Hemisphere the Moon appears rotated compared with the Northern Hemisphere, and near the equator its orientation changes as it crosses the sky. Learn **waxing** and **waning** from the sequence, not from a rule that says the bright side must always be left or right.
</Callout>

## Make the phases with a ball and lamp

This model lets your own head become Earth, a ball become the Moon and a lamp become the Sun. You will see the light change for exactly the same geometric reason that the real phases change in the sky.

### What you need

- **A small matte white ball.** A polystyrene craft ball on a pencil is easiest to hold, but a table-tennis ball, pale orange or ball of crumpled white paper also works.
- **A desk lamp with a cool LED bulb**, ideally with the shade removed or pointed so the light reaches the whole ball. A strong torch fixed in one place can work too.
- **A room you can darken.** The darker the room, the clearer the boundary between lunar day and night.
- **Enough clear floor space to turn around** with one arm stretched out.

<Callout type="safety" title="Use a lamp, never the Sun">
Do not stare at the real Sun or use it as the light source for this indoor model. Use a cool LED rather than a hot incandescent bulb, keep the lamp stable and have an adult arrange cables so nobody trips while turning.
</Callout>

### Build the model

1. Put the lamp at about head height and stand two or three steps away. Darken the room and switch on your model Sun.
2. Hold the ball at arm's length. Keep your face pointed toward the ball throughout the activity: you are an observer standing on Earth, always looking at the Moon.
3. Begin facing the lamp, with the ball a little below the direct line between your eyes and the bulb. The illuminated side faces away from you. This is the **new Moon** position.
4. Turn slowly to your left, moving the ball with you. Stop after about one eighth of a turn. A bright crescent should appear.
5. Continue in eighth-turn steps. At a quarter turn you should see half of the ball lit; halfway around you should see a full Moon; three quarters around gives the opposite half.
6. When your back faces the lamp, lift the ball a little above your head. Otherwise your head will cast a shadow on it and accidentally turn the model into a lunar eclipse.
7. Complete the turn until the ball is back near the lamp. Name each phase as it appears, then repeat the orbit without looking at the list.

The model is deliberately not to scale. If your head were a correctly sized Earth, the model Moon would be about one quarter as wide and roughly thirty Earth-widths away. Your arm is much too short, but the light geometry still works.

## Why Earth's shadow is not the answer

The shadow explanation feels convincing because the dark portion of the Moon looks like a shadow. It **is** shadow, but almost always it is the Moon's own night side, not Earth's shadow falling on it.

The Moon's orbit is tilted by about five degrees compared with Earth's path around the Sun. At most new and full Moons, the three bodies are not lined up exactly: the Moon passes a little above or below the straight Sun-Earth line.

Only when the alignment is unusually precise do we get an eclipse:

- At **new Moon**, the Moon can pass directly in front of the Sun and cast its shadow on Earth. That is a **solar eclipse**.
- At **full Moon**, the Moon can pass through Earth's shadow. That is a **lunar eclipse**.

If Earth's shadow caused the phases, a lunar eclipse would happen every month. It does not.

## Why the cycle takes 29.5 days

The Moon takes about **27.3 days** to travel once around Earth compared with the distant stars. Yet the time from one new Moon to the next is about **29.5 days**.

The extra two days appear because Earth is moving too. While the Moon completes its orbit, Earth and Moon have travelled further along their shared path around the Sun. The Moon needs a little additional time to catch up and reach the same Sun-Earth-Moon arrangement again.

This 29.5-day phase cycle is sometimes called a **synodic month**. It is the reason our word *month* is so closely related to *Moon*.

## The phase tells you when to look

The Moon is not only a night-time object. Its place in the orbit also changes roughly when it rises and sets.

- A **waxing crescent** is easiest to find after sunset.
- A **first-quarter Moon** rises around midday and can remain visible into the first half of the night.
- A **full Moon** rises around sunset and sets around sunrise.
- A **last-quarter Moon** rises around midnight and is often visible in the morning sky.
- A **waning crescent** is best looked for before sunrise and may remain visible after daylight begins.

These are useful approximations, not appointments. Exact times depend on the date and your location.

<Callout type="tip" title="🔭 The full Moon is not always the best Moon">
A full Moon is bright, but its nearly overhead sunlight makes craters look flat. Around first or last quarter, long shadows beside crater rims and mountains make the surface relief much easier to see, even without a telescope.
</Callout>

## Keep a Moon journal

For a real investigation, observe the Moon on at least seven clear days spread across two or three weeks.

Each time, record:

- the date and local time;
- where the Moon is in the sky;
- a simple drawing of the illuminated shape;
- whether it is waxing or waning;
- one detail you notice, such as earthshine, a nearby bright planet or visible surface markings.

Try some daytime observations as well. The Moon generally appears further east among the background stars each day and rises later as it moves along its orbit. Missing a cloudy evening is not a failed experiment; record the cloud and continue on the next clear day.

## What this model can and cannot show

A model is useful precisely because it simplifies. The lamp and ball show the most important relationship: **one light source, a half-lit Moon and a moving viewpoint**.

They do not show the true sizes or distances. They exaggerate how much you have to raise the ball to avoid your head's shadow. They also cannot show your view from Earth and an astronaut's view from above the orbit at the same time. That two-view problem is where an interactive model is especially useful.

<KeyTakeaways>
- The Moon is a sphere and does not physically change shape during the month.
- The Moon does not make its own light; **moonlight is reflected sunlight**.
- The Sun ordinarily illuminates **half of the Moon** at all times.
- Phases happen because the Moon's orbit changes how much of that bright half we can see from Earth.
- **Waxing** means the visible illuminated part is growing; **waning** means it is shrinking.
- Earth's shadow causes a **lunar eclipse**, not the ordinary monthly phases.
- The eight named phases repeat in about **29.5 days**.
- Everyone experiences the same phase, but its orientation looks different from different parts of Earth.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Moon phases - Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="What causes the phases of the Moon?">
The Sun always illuminates half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, our viewing angle changes, so we see different amounts of that illuminated half. Those changing views are the phases.
</Question>
<Question question="Are Moon phases caused by Earth's shadow?">
No. Earth's shadow only darkens the Moon during a **lunar eclipse**. During ordinary phases we are seeing different proportions of the Moon's own day side and night side.
</Question>
<Question question="What are the eight Moon phases in order?">
New Moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full Moon, waning gibbous, last quarter and waning crescent. The transition is continuous even though we give these eight points names.
</Question>
<Question question="Why is first quarter called a quarter when half the Moon looks bright?">
Because the Moon has completed about one quarter of its phase cycle. The name describes its position in the cycle, not the fraction of the visible disk that is illuminated. At first quarter, half of the disk looks bright.
</Question>
<Question question="How long does a complete Moon phase cycle take?">
About **29.5 days** from one new Moon to the next. The Moon completes an orbit relative to the stars in about 27.3 days, but it needs a little longer to return to the same alignment with the moving Earth and Sun.
</Question>
<Question question="Can you see the Moon during the day?">
Yes. The Moon spends about as much time above the horizon during daylight as it does at night, although glare can make it harder to notice. Crescents, quarter Moons and gibbous Moons are commonly visible in a blue sky.
</Question>
<Question question="Do people everywhere see the same Moon phase?">
Yes, apart from the tiny change that occurs while light and observers move. But people in different hemispheres see the Moon rotated at different angles, so the illuminated portion may appear on the opposite side.
</Question>
<Question question="What age is the ball-and-lamp Moon activity for?">
Children from about **age 8** can usually follow the changing light with adult guidance. Understanding the three-dimensional Sun-Earth-Moon geometry may take several repetitions, and that is normal. Younger children can still observe and name the shapes without mastering the full model.
</Question>
</FAQ>
