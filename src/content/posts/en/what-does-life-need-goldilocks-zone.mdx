There are somewhere around a trillion kinds of living thing on Earth, from the bacteria in a puddle to the blue whale. They live in deserts, in ice, in caves, on your skin. They look nothing like each other.

And yet every single one of them needs the same short list of things. That list is the most useful thing we own when we go looking for life somewhere else, because we cannot search a whole galaxy at random. We have to know what to look for.

<Callout type="info" title="🧒 In one sentence">
Life needs liquid water, a source of energy, the right atoms, some shelter and a great deal of time, and the easiest place to find all five at once is a planet sitting at just the right distance from its star.
</Callout>

## The five things on the list

<Figure src="/images/posts/life-needs-en.png" alt="Five cards in a row showing what living things need: a blue water drop for liquid water, an orange lightning bolt for an energy source, a green flask for the right atoms, a purple shield for shelter from radiation, and a red hourglass for a lot of time" width="1500" height="640">
Every living thing we have ever found needs all five. Take one away and, as far as we know, nothing gets started.
</Figure>

### 1. Liquid water

Not ice. Not steam. **Liquid** water, the kind you can pour.

Living things are essentially very complicated chemistry sets, and chemistry needs somewhere for the parts to bump into each other. In ice, everything is locked in place and nothing can move. In steam, everything flies apart. Liquid water is the in-between state where molecules can drift, meet and react.

Water is also astonishingly good at dissolving things, which is why it is nicknamed the **universal solvent**. It carries food into a cell and carries waste back out. If your explorer has not yet met water's stranger habits, [the amazing properties of water](/en/the-amazing-properties-of-water) has experiments you can run at the kitchen sink this afternoon.

### 2. A source of energy

Nothing stays alive for free. Every living thing has to keep spending energy just to stay organised, the way a tidy room only stays tidy if somebody keeps tidying it.

Most life on Earth runs on **sunlight**. Plants catch it, animals eat the plants, and so on up the chain. But sunlight is not the only option, and this turns out to matter enormously. At the bottom of the ocean, kilometres below the last scrap of daylight, whole crowded communities live around **hydrothermal vents**, cracks where hot water loaded with chemicals comes pouring out of the sea floor. Nothing down there has ever seen the Sun. The bacteria at the base of that food web run on chemistry instead of light.

### 3. The right atoms

Life is built mostly from six ingredients: **carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulphur**. Carbon does the heavy lifting, because a carbon atom can link up with four others at once and build chains, rings and structures of enormous complexity. No other common atom is anywhere near as sociable.

The good news is that these atoms are not rare. They were forged inside stars and scattered everywhere when those stars died, which means the raw material for life is lying around all over the galaxy. Your body is made of it.

### 4. Some shelter

Space is a violent place. Radiation from stars would tear apart the delicate molecules life is built from, so life needs something between it and the sky. On Earth that job is done by our **atmosphere** and our **magnetic field**, which together soak up or deflect most of what comes at us.

But shelter does not have to mean air. A few metres of rock works. So does a thick layer of ice. That loophole will matter later.

### 5. A great deal of time

Life on Earth took hundreds of millions of years to get started and billions more to become anything you could see without a microscope. That only works if conditions stay reasonably steady for a very long stretch. A world that swings wildly between boiling and freezing every few thousand years never gets the chance.

## So where do you find all five at once?

Here is the trick astronomers use. Four of the five are common. The fussy one is **liquid water**, because water is only liquid across a fairly narrow band of temperatures, and temperature mostly depends on one thing: how far you are from your star.

Too close and your oceans boil away. Too far and they freeze solid. In between there is a ring at just the right distance, and that ring has a name every child recognises instantly.

<Figure src="/images/posts/habitable-zone-en.png" alt="A diagram of the Sun with a wide band running away from it, split into three coloured zones: a dark red too hot zone containing Mercury at 0.39 AU and Venus at 0.72 AU, a green just right zone containing Earth at 1 AU, and a blue too cold zone containing Mars at 1.52 AU" width="1500" height="900">
The green ring is the Goldilocks zone: not too hot, not too cold. Earth sits comfortably inside it. Mars misses the edge by a surprisingly small margin.
</Figure>

The **Goldilocks zone**, or the habitable zone if you want the formal name, is the band around a star where a planet can hold liquid water on its surface. Not too hot, not too cold, just right.

Bigger, hotter stars have their Goldilocks zone much further out. Small, cool red stars have theirs squeezed in tight, so a planet would have to huddle very close to stay warm. Every star has one. The question is always whether anything happens to be sitting in it.

## Being in the zone is not enough

The Goldilocks zone is a starting point, not a promise. Plenty of things can sit in it and still be lifeless.

The clearest example is hanging in our sky right now. **The Moon** orbits at the same distance from the Sun as we do, squarely inside the zone, and it is bone dry and dead. It is too small for its gravity to hold on to an atmosphere, so any water simply drifted away long ago.

Mars tells a similar story. It sits just past the outer edge, and it has the further problem that its air is about a hundred times thinner than ours. But we can see dried-up riverbeds, ancient lake floors and minerals that only form in water, so Mars almost certainly had rivers and lakes billions of years ago. Then it lost most of its atmosphere, and the water went with it.

<Callout type="science" title="🔬 What we are really looking for">
When a telescope studies a distant planet, it is not looking for cities or trees. It is looking at the **light that filters through that planet's air**, because each gas absorbs its own particular colours and leaves a fingerprint. Oxygen and methane together are the interesting combination, since they react with each other and should not both last long unless something keeps topping them up. On Earth, that something is life.
</Callout>

## The creatures that break all the rules

Now for the part that changed how scientists search.

For a long time the rules seemed sensible. Then biologists started looking in places nobody expected anything to live, and kept finding things living there anyway. We call them **extremophiles**, which simply means "lovers of extremes".

- **In boiling water.** *Methanopyrus kandleri* lives at 122 °C near deep-sea vents. Not survives. Lives, grows and multiplies.
- **In acid that would dissolve metal.** Some microbes thrive in pools with a pH near zero, roughly the acidity of battery acid.
- **Under crushing radiation.** *Deinococcus radiodurans* shrugs off doses of radiation thousands of times higher than would kill you, by stitching its own shredded DNA back together.
- **Kilometres down inside solid rock.** There are bacteria living in the dark deep inside Earth's crust, eating chemicals out of the stone, whose generations may pass on a scale of centuries.
- **In dried-out suspended animation.** **Tardigrades**, the eight-legged "water bears" your explorer can find in a scrap of damp moss, can pull in their legs, expel almost all their water and become a tun: a dormant husk that has survived being frozen, boiled, crushed and even carried into the vacuum of space. They are not living while in that state. They simply refuse to be finished off, and start again when water returns.

<Callout type="tip" title="🔍 Try this: find a tardigrade">
Take a clump of moss off a wall, soak it in a saucer of water overnight, then squeeze the water out and look at a drop under a microscope at around 40 to 100 times magnification. Tardigrades are common, and they are surprisingly charming to watch trundling about. This is the most reliable way there is to show a child a creature that has genuinely been to space and come back.
</Callout>

## The loophole: oceans in the dark

Put the extremophiles together with the hydrothermal vents and you get an idea that has redirected an entire field.

If life can run on chemistry instead of sunlight, and a thick layer of ice counts as shelter, then a world does **not** need to be in the Goldilocks zone at all. It just needs liquid water somewhere, and a way to keep it warm.

There is a way. When a moon orbits a giant planet, the planet's gravity stretches and squeezes it, and all that flexing generates heat from the inside. It is the same reason a paperclip gets warm if you bend it back and forth quickly.

Two places in our own Solar System look extremely interesting because of it, and neither is anywhere near the Goldilocks zone:

**Europa**, a moon of Jupiter, is wrapped in a shell of ice with a salty ocean underneath. That ocean is thought to hold more liquid water than every ocean on Earth put together.

**Enceladus**, a small moon of Saturn, fires jets of water out through cracks near its south pole. The Cassini spacecraft flew straight through those plumes and tasted them, finding salt, silica and organic molecules: exactly what you would expect from warm water in contact with rock.

Neither of those is proof of anything living. But both mean the honest answer to "where should we look?" has grown much larger than a neat green ring.

## Are we alone?

Nobody knows. That is the honest answer, and it is worth giving a child plainly rather than dressing it up.

What we can say is that we now know of **more than 6,000 planets around other stars**, and the count keeps climbing. Some of them are rocky, roughly Earth-sized, and sitting in their star's Goldilocks zone. The nearest of those orbits Proxima Centauri, our closest neighbouring star, a mere 4.2 light years away.

We have gone, within a single lifetime, from not knowing whether other planets existed at all to arguing about which ones to point our best telescope at first. That is a remarkable place to have arrived, and your explorer gets to find out what happens next.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Every living thing we know of needs **five things**: liquid water, an energy source, the right atoms, shelter from radiation, and a lot of steady time.
- The fussiest one is **liquid water**, which only exists across a narrow range of temperatures.
- The **Goldilocks zone** is the ring around a star where a planet can keep liquid water on its surface: not too hot, not too cold.
- Being in the zone is **not enough**. The Moon is in it and is dead, because it is too small to hold an atmosphere.
- A planet's **atmosphere** matters as much as its distance. Venus is hotter than Mercury because its thick carbon dioxide air traps the heat.
- **Extremophiles** on Earth live in boiling water, in acid, under intense radiation and deep inside rock, which widened where we think life is possible.
- Moons like **Europa** and **Enceladus** may hold liquid oceans under ice, heated from inside, far outside any Goldilocks zone.
- We know of more than **6,000 planets** around other stars, and some are rocky and in the zone.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="What is the Goldilocks zone, in simple terms?">
It is the ring around a star where a planet is at the right distance to keep **liquid water** on its surface. Closer in, the water boils away. Further out, it freezes. The name comes from the fairy tale: not too hot, not too cold, just right. Astronomers also call it the **habitable zone**.
</Question>
<Question question="Why does life need water rather than some other liquid?">
Because water does several jobs at once. It dissolves an unusually wide range of substances, so it can carry nutrients in and waste out. It stays liquid across a useful span of temperatures. And it takes a lot of heat to warm it up or cool it down, which steadies the temperature of anything made largely of water, including you. Other liquids are possible in theory, but water is the one we know works.
</Question>
<Question question="Is Earth the only planet in the Sun's Goldilocks zone?">
Yes, on the strict definition. Mars sits just past the outer edge and Venus just inside the inner edge, which is exactly why both are interesting: they show what happens to an almost-right planet that gets it slightly wrong in each direction.
</Question>
<Question question="Could there be life on Mars?">
Not on the surface as it is today: the air is far too thin and the radiation too harsh. But Mars clearly had rivers and lakes billions of years ago, so the real question is whether life started back then, and whether anything might still hang on underground where there is ice. Several rovers are looking for exactly those traces right now.
</Question>
<Question question="What is an extremophile?">
A living thing that thrives in conditions we would consider impossible: boiling water, near-freezing brine, crushing pressure, acid, or intense radiation. They matter because they widened our idea of where life can exist. Before we found them, an icy moon with no sunlight looked hopeless. Now it looks like somewhere worth visiting.
</Question>
<Question question="How do we look for life on planets around other stars?">
Mostly by studying the **starlight that passes through a planet's atmosphere** as it crosses in front of its star. Each gas absorbs its own colours, so the missing colours tell us what the air is made of. Certain combinations, such as oxygen alongside methane, are hard to explain without something continually producing them.
</Question>
<Question question="What age is this topic good for?">
The five requirements work from about **age 5** if you keep it to water, food, air and time. The Goldilocks zone lands well at **7 and up**, especially with a drawing of the three zones. Extremophiles and the icy-moon oceans suit **9 and up**, and tend to be the part that sticks.
</Question>
</FAQ>
