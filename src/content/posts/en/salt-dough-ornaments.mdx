Salt dough is flour, salt and water baked low and slow until all the water has gone, and the salt keeps mould away so your ornaments last for years.

Salt dough ornaments are one of those crafts that families keep forever. A star a three-year-old cut out, a handprint from the year a baby turned one, a lopsided heart with "Grandma" written on the back. Every December they come out of the box again, and remind us of the good times.

They're also a great piece of kitchen science. Why don't they go mouldy like bread? Why do we bake them at such a low temperature? And how much water is hiding in the dough? These are the perfect questions to start exploring.

Salt dough ornaments are doors 5 and 6 in our [STEM Advent Calendar](/en/stem-advent-calendar): make and dry them on Saturday, paint them on Sunday.

## Materials

**For the dough (makes about 15 ornaments):**

- 250 g (2 cups) plain flour
- 125 g (about ½ cup) fine salt
- About 150 ml (⅔ cup) lukewarm water
- 1 tablespoon cooking oil (optional, makes the dough smoother)

**For shaping and decorating:**

- A rolling pin and cookie cutters (stars, hearts, trees, bells)
- A drinking straw for the hanging holes
- Baking paper and a baking tray
- Acrylic or poster paints, brushes, a white paint pen
- Clear varnish, or PVA glue mixed with a little water, to seal
- Ribbon or string

## How to make salt dough ornaments

1. **Mix the dry ingredients.** Stir the flour and salt together in a large bowl.
2. **Add the water gradually.** Pour in about two thirds of the water and the oil, mix, then add the rest a little at a time until a dough forms. Too sticky? Add a spoonful of flour. Too crumbly? A few drops of water.
3. **Knead for 5 minutes** until the dough is smooth and soft, like playdough. Children love this part.
4. **Roll it out** on baking paper to about **5 mm** thick. Thinner ornaments dry faster; much thicker ones take ages and are more likely to crack.
5. **Cut out shapes** with cookie cutters and lift them onto a baking tray lined with baking paper.
6. **Make a hanging hole** near the top of each one with a drinking straw. Make it a little bigger than you think you need.
7. **Add texture** if you like: press in a fork, a leaf, a piece of lace or a button, then lift it off.

### Handprint keepsakes

Roll a ball of dough the size of an orange, flatten it into a circle about 1 cm thick and press your child's hand firmly into the middle. Write the name and year in the dough with a toothpick. Handprints are thicker than the other ornaments, so they need longer to dry.

## Drying: oven or air

**In the oven:** bake at **100 to 120 °C** (fan off) for about **2 to 3 hours** for 5 mm ornaments, turning them over halfway. Handprints and thick shapes can need 3 to 4 hours or more. They're done when they're hard all the way through and sound hollow when you tap them. Turn the oven off and let them cool inside with the door open a crack.

**In the air:** leave them on a rack in a warm, dry room for **2 to 4 days**, turning them twice a day so both sides dry.

<Callout type="safety">
Salt dough isn't food. It's very salty, and eating a lot of it can make a child seriously ill. It's even more dangerous for **dogs**, who are attracted to the smell, so hang finished ornaments up high and keep the dough away from pets. An adult should handle the hot oven and trays.
</Callout>

## Painting and sealing your ornaments

Wait until the ornaments are completely dry and cool. Then:

1. **Paint** them with acrylic or poster paint. Let the first colour dry before adding details.
2. **Add details** with a white paint pen, a toothpick dipped in paint, or glitter glue.
3. **Seal them** on both sides and around the edges with clear varnish or a thin coat of PVA glue mixed with a little water. Let them dry, then thread the ribbon through.

Sealing isn't just for shine. Salt soaks up moisture from the air, so unsealed ornaments stored in a damp place can slowly go soft again. Sealed and stored in a box with tissue paper in a dry cupboard, they last for many years.

### Make Croatian-style licitar hearts

In northern Croatia, bright red heart-shaped biscuits called **licitars** have been a symbol of love, fairs and Christmas for centuries. They're made from sweet honey dough, painted red and decorated with white icing patterns, small mirrors and little verses. The gingerbread craft of northern Croatia is even on UNESCO's list of the world's intangible cultural heritage.

You can make salt dough hearts in the same style: paint them bright red, add rows of white dots and swirly lines along the edges with a white paint pen, and glue a small round mirror sticker or a circle of foil in the middle. Write a short message or a name in white on the back.

## The science: why salt?

### Salt keeps the mould away

A plain flour-and-water dough would go mouldy within days. Mould and bacteria need water to live and grow. When there's a lot of salt around them, water is pulled out of their cells and into the salty surroundings, a process called <Term def="Water moving through a thin barrier, like a cell wall, from where there is less dissolved stuff to where there is more.">osmosis</Term>. The microbes dry out and can't grow.

People have used this trick for thousands of years: salted fish, salted ham and pickles keep for a long time for exactly the same reason.

There's also a lot more salt in the dough than the water can dissolve. That's why salt dough feels slightly grainy: some of the salt is still there as tiny crystals.

### Why bake it so low?

You're not really *baking* salt dough. You're **drying** it. At a normal baking temperature, the water inside turns to steam so quickly that it can't escape fast enough. The steam pushes the dough up into bubbles, and the ornament puffs, cracks and goes brown. At a low temperature, the water leaves gently from the surface and the ornament stays flat and pale.

<Callout type="tip" title="🔬 Make it a real experiment">
Weigh two identical ornaments on a kitchen scale and write the weights down. Dry one in the oven and leave one to air-dry. Weigh the air-dried one every morning. ### Bonus experiment: salt against mould

For curious older children: make two small balls of dough, one with salt and one with only flour and water. Put each into its own small jar with a few drops of water, close the lids tightly and label them. Watch them for a week **without opening the jars**. Which one grows fuzzy mould spots? When you're done, throw both jars away with the lids still on, because mould spores aren't good to breathe in.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Salt dough is **250 g flour, 125 g fine salt and about 150 ml water**, rolled 5 mm thick.
- Dry it at **100 to 120 °C for 2 to 3 hours**, or in the air for 2 to 4 days. Thick handprints need longer.
- Salt pulls water out of mould and bacteria by **osmosis**, so the ornaments don't go mouldy.
- A low temperature lets the water leave gently. A hot oven turns it into **steam** that puffs and cracks the dough.
- Seal the ornaments, because salt soaks up moisture from the air. Keep them away from **pets**: salt dough isn't food.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="What is the recipe for salt dough ornaments?">
Mix 250 g (2 cups) of plain flour with 125 g (about ½ cup) of fine salt, then add about 150 ml (⅔ cup) of lukewarm water gradually and knead for 5 minutes until smooth. A tablespoon of oil makes the dough a little smoother.
</Question>
<Question question="How long do you bake salt dough ornaments?">
At 100 to 120 °C, ornaments 5 mm thick need about 2 to 3 hours. Turn them over halfway. Thick shapes such as handprints can take 3 to 4 hours or longer. They're ready when they're hard all the way through.
</Question>
<Question question="Can salt dough air-dry instead?">
Yes. Leave the ornaments on a rack in a warm, dry room for 2 to 4 days and turn them twice a day. Air-drying takes longer but avoids any puffing or browning.
</Question>
<Question question="Why did my salt dough ornaments puff up or crack?">
The oven was too hot, so the water inside turned to steam too quickly. Keep the oven at 100 to 120 °C, roll the dough no thicker than about 5 mm, and dry thick shapes for longer.
</Question>
<Question question="How long do salt dough ornaments last?">
Many years, if they're completely dry, sealed with varnish or diluted PVA glue, and stored in a dry place. Unsealed ornaments can absorb moisture from the air and go soft.
</Question>
<Question question="Are salt dough ornaments safe for pets?">
No. The high salt content is dangerous for dogs in particular, who may try to eat them. Hang ornaments out of reach and call a vet if your pet eats one.
</Question>
</FAQ>
