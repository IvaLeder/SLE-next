A tower of blocks falls over. The child who built it is furious, or in tears, and the adult nearby says something kind about trying again.

An engineer would have said something else entirely: *where did it go first?*

That question is not a trick for making a child feel better. It is the actual working method of the profession. Engineers expect the first version to fail, and they build it partly to find out how. The whole job is arranged as a loop that goes back round every time something breaks, and that loop is simple enough for a six-year-old to follow and honest enough that university engineering courses teach the same five steps.

## What engineers actually do all day

There is a tidy way to separate the two big STEM jobs, and children get it immediately:

> **A scientist asks why the world is the way it is. An engineer asks how to make something the world does not have yet.**

That difference changes what counts as a right answer. A science question has one, eventually. An engineering question never does. There is no correct bridge. There is a bridge that is cheaper, one that is stronger, one that is quicker to build, one that is prettier, and choosing between them is a **trade-off** rather than a calculation. When your child says "but which way is the best way?", the honest answer is "best at what?", and that is not a dodge. It is the first genuinely engineering-shaped thought they will have.

## The five steps

The version taught in schools has five steps. Different books rename them, but they are the same loop underneath.

### 1. Ask: what is the problem, exactly?

Before any building: who is this for, what has to happen, and how will we know it worked?

Most childhood projects go wrong here rather than later. "Build a bridge" is not a problem, it is a shape. "Build something that gets a toy car from this chair to that chair without touching the floor, using only newspaper and tape" is a problem: it says what has to happen, it says what you may use, and it can be checked.

The most useful habit to teach at this step is naming **what counts as working**, out loud, before starting. It should be something you could actually measure. Holds a full cup of water. Carries ten coins. Rolls three metres. Fits through the doorway.

### 2. Imagine: what are all the ways this could be done?

Three ideas, not one.

This is the step everybody skips, adults included, because the first idea arrives with such confidence. But the first idea is usually just the most obvious one, and you cannot tell whether it is any good until there is a second and a third standing next to it. Asking for a deliberately silly third idea works well: it loosens up the thinking, and often the silly one turns out to contain something useful.

Look at how other people solved it too. That is not cheating. An engineer who ignored every bridge ever built would be a very bad engineer.

### 3. Plan: how will this one actually go together?

Pick one idea and draw it big. Label the parts. Write down what you need and how much of it.

Drawing feels like a delay to a child holding a roll of tape, and it is worth insisting on anyway, for one reason that they understand at once: **a drawing is much cheaper to change than a build.** Move a line on paper and you have lost two seconds. Move a wall after gluing it and you have lost the afternoon.

One question to add to the plan, which is the single best thing on the printable notebook: *which part are you least sure about?* Every design has one. Naming it in advance is what separates a plan from a hope, and it also tells you where to look first when the thing gives way.

### 4. Create: build it and test it properly

Build it, then test it in a way you can trust.

"Properly" mostly means **the same way every time**. If the first version was tested by dropping a book on it from waist height and the second one by pressing gently with a hand, you have learned nothing about which version is better. Same drop, same height, same book. Add weight one coin at a time rather than a whole handful. Start the car from the same mark on the ramp.

Write down what happened while it is fresh. Children are certain they will remember and they do not.

### 5. Improve: what broke, and what will you change?

Find the weakest part. Change **one** thing. Test again.

One thing, because if you rebuild the deck and thicken the legs and add a diagonal all at once and it now holds, you have no idea which of the three did it. Real engineers are strict about this and it is the fastest habit to teach: it just means going round the loop more often, in smaller steps.

Then round again. And again. The loop has no finish line: a design is finished when you decide to stop improving it, and every engineer stops somewhere.

## Testing is a skill of its own

Two ideas hide inside step four, and both are worth pulling out because they are the same ideas school science lessons will formalise later.

**Deciding what counts as working.** A vague goal cannot be tested, so it cannot be improved. "Make it good" gives you nothing to do next. "Hold five coins in the middle without the deck touching the table" gives you a number that either went up or did not.

**Changing one thing at a time.** This is the fair test, and children accept it readily when it is framed as a puzzle about credit: if you changed three things and it improved, which one deserves the medal?

<Callout type="science" title="Engineering is where the constraints are">
Ask a child to build the strongest bridge they can and you will get an enormous mound of tape. Ask for the strongest bridge from **one sheet of newspaper and 30 cm of tape** and you get engineering, because now every decision costs something. Constraints are not a way of making the task harder for its own sake. They are what forces the interesting thinking, and they are why professional engineering is mostly about budgets, weights, deadlines and materials rather than about what would be nicest.
</Callout>

## When it falls over

This is the part that decides whether a child does it again, and it lands almost entirely on what the nearby adult says in the first ten seconds.

**Ask where it went, not why it went.** "Where did it break first?" is answerable, it sends the child back to look, and it turns a collapse into evidence. "Why did you build it like that?" sounds like a verdict.

**Say what a test is for.** A test that everything survives has told you almost nothing. The bridge that folded on the second coin has told you exactly where the weak point is. That is not a consolation prize; it is genuinely the more useful of the two outcomes, and children can tell when an adult means it.

**Keep the broken one.** Line up version one, version two and version three. The progress is invisible in the moment and obvious on a shelf.

**Let it be their design, including the bad bits.** The urge to reach in and fix the wobbly corner is strong. A design a child has improved themselves is worth more than a good design handed to them, and the improving is the entire lesson.

**Watch your own language about the first try.** "It didn't work" and "the first version told us the legs are too thin" describe the same afternoon.

## Run the loop on something small this weekend

Pick a build your child already wants to do and add the notebook to it. These work particularly well because each one has an obvious way to measure success and an obvious thing to improve:

- **[A bridge that holds coins](/en/why-triangles-are-the-strongest-shape)** - the clearest measurement of all, a number that goes up. Test straws with no diagonals, then with diagonals, and watch the count climb.
- **[A catapult](/en/make-popsicle-catapult)** - distance is a lovely thing to measure. Change one thing per round: the arm length, the number of elastic bands, the angle.
- **[A cardboard castle](/en/toilet-roll-craft-cardboard-castle)** - the trade-off arrives on its own here, between tall and stable, and it is a good one to name out loud.
- **[A paper windmill](/en/how-to-make-paper-windmill)** - test with a fan at a fixed distance, then change the size or the angle of the blades.
- **[A potato battery](/en/how-to-make-potato-battery)** - fewer things to build, more things to vary: how many potatoes, which metals, how far apart.

<Callout type="tip" title="🔬 A 30-minute version, if you have nothing prepared">
The classic: build the tallest tower you can that holds a marshmallow on top, using 20 sticks of dry spaghetti, one metre of tape and one metre of string, in 18 minutes. Run it twice with the same materials. The second attempt is nearly always better, and the reason why is the whole article: they spent the first attempt finding out how it fails.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Engineering is a **loop**: ask, imagine, plan, create, improve, then round again. It is not a straight line, and it has no finish line.
- **Science asks why, engineering asks how.** An engineering question has no single right answer, only trade-offs between better and worse.
- Say **what counts as working** before you start, in something you can measure. A vague goal cannot be tested or improved.
- Have **three ideas** before choosing one, and draw the chosen one before building it. Paper is cheaper to change than glue.
- Test the **same way every time**, and change **one thing** between rounds, or you will not know what helped.
- The **improve** step is where professionals spend most of their time. A build that fails has produced data, and it is the most useful outcome a first attempt can have.
</KeyTakeaways>

## The engineering design process - Frequently Asked Questions

<FAQ>
<Question question="What is the engineering design process?">
It is the loop engineers work in: ask what the problem is, imagine several possible solutions, plan one of them, create and test it, then improve it and go round again. The steps are sometimes named differently (define, brainstorm, prototype, evaluate), but the shape is always the same: it repeats, and it treats a failed test as information rather than as an ending.
</Question>
<Question question="What are the 5 steps of the engineering design process for kids?">
Ask (what is the problem, exactly, and what counts as working?), Imagine (what are all the ways I could do this?), Plan (draw it big and list what I need), Create (build it and test it the same way every time), and Improve (find the weakest part, change one thing, test again). Then straight back to the beginning.
</Question>
<Question question="What is the difference between science and engineering?">
A scientist investigates why the world behaves as it does and is looking for an answer that is true. An engineer makes something that does not exist yet and is looking for a solution that is good enough, given the materials, time and money available. Science has right answers; engineering has trade-offs.
</Question>
<Question question="At what age can children use the engineering design process?">
From about 4 or 5 in a spoken version: what are we making, what shall we try, did it work, what shall we change? Writing it down starts working around 7. By 9 or 10 children can hold a fair test in mind on their own, name the part they are least sure about in advance, and keep a proper log across several rounds.
</Question>
<Question question="What should I say when my child's project collapses?">
Ask where it gave way first, rather than why they built it that way. The first question sends them back to look and turns the collapse into evidence; the second sounds like a judgment. Then ask what one thing they would change, and try again. Keeping the broken version next to the new one makes the progress visible.
</Question>
<Question question="Why should you only change one thing at a time?">
Because otherwise you cannot tell which change helped. If a child thickens the legs, adds a diagonal and shortens the span all at once and the bridge now holds, three explanations fit equally well and they have learned nothing they can reuse. Changing one thing per round means more rounds, but every round teaches something.
</Question>
<Question question="Do I need special materials to teach this?">
No, and simple materials are better. Newspaper, tape, dry spaghetti, drinking straws, cardboard boxes and string are enough for everything on this page. What produces the engineering thinking is not the material but the limit on it: a fixed amount of tape forces choices in a way that an unlimited roll never will.
</Question>
</FAQ>
